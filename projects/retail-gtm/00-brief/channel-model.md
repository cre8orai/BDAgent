# The channel model

Who the intermediaries are, what each actually wants, and why Cre8or answers a problem
they already have. These segments are hypotheses until `01-research/` confirms them —
but they are specific enough to be falsified, which is the point.

## The pattern that unites every segment

Each of these partners owns **demand** — an audience, an IP right, a client
relationship, a shelf. None of them owns **supply**. Today they either don't offer
beauty at all, or they broker it badly through a conventional manufacturer with a
5,000-unit minimum and a four-month lead time.

The offer is the same sentence in every segment, with one noun swapped:

> You already have the audience. We make the product line, at whatever volume that
> audience actually turns out to be — starting at 12 units and scaling to 50,000
> without changing anything.

## Segment A — Licensing & merchandise aggregators

**Who:** Fanatics, Bravado (UMG), WMG merch, Live Nation Merchandise, league and team
licensing arms, studio consumer-products divisions.

**What they own:** Licensing rights to talent, teams and franchises. Existing merch
supply chains for apparel and hard goods. Venue, tour and stadium retail. Deep
first-party fan data.

**Their problem:** Merch demand is event-shaped and violently unpredictable — a tour, a
playoff run, an album drop. Apparel they can handle; they've done it for decades. Beauty
they largely can't, because cosmetic MOQs force a volume bet months before anyone knows
whether the thing is a hit, and unsold cosmetics carry shelf-life and disposal problems
that unsold t-shirts don't.

**Why Cre8or:** The strongest fit in the model, and the reason this project exists.
On-demand production converts an inventory bet into a variable cost. Beauty margins beat
apparel margins. And a beauty SKU gives them something new to sell a fan who already
owns three shirts.

**The canonical deal:** Fanatics stands up a Taylor Swift beauty line for a stadium tour.
Cre8or manufactures and fulfils. Fanatics owns the artist relationship, the creative and
the retail. Volume follows actual per-city sell-through instead of a forecast.

`[OPEN]` Licensor approval chains are slow and risk-averse. Cosmetics touch skin, which
triggers safety and liability review a hoodie never does. Understand this gate before
building the pitch — it may *be* the deal.

## Segment B — Creative, brand & e-commerce agencies

**Who:** Brand-building agencies, DTC growth shops, Shopify Plus partner agencies,
influencer and creator agencies that launch talent-fronted brands.

**What they own:** The client relationship and the creative. Often the entire strategy
for a celebrity or creator brand launch.

**Their problem:** They can design a beauty brand beautifully, then must hand the client
to a manufacturer — where the project stalls, margin leaks to someone else, and the
agency's revenue caps out at a project fee.

**Why Cre8or:** Turns an agency from a service vendor into a product business. They keep
the client, add a manufacturing margin, and can finally say yes to "can you actually
make this?"

An internal doc — *"The Cre8or & Agency Revolution"* — already exists and should be
extracted into `01-research/` before this positioning is finalised. The Cre8or Shopify
app is a natural wedge into the Shopify agency ecosystem.

## Segment C — Talent, creator & celebrity management

**Who:** Talent management firms, creator networks and MCNs, celebrity brand incubators.

**What they own:** The talent, and the audience the talent brings.

**Their problem:** Every client wants a beauty line. Most clients don't have the audience
size to justify a conventional manufacturing run, so most lines never get made — or get
made badly, and a bad first launch burns the talent's credibility.

**Why Cre8or:** Makes the mid-tier creator launch economically possible for the first
time. Seed at 12 units, scale only if it sells. **Failure gets cheap**, which means far
more launches can be attempted — and a management firm's portfolio economics change
entirely when the downside of a miss is small.

## Segment D — Brand aggregators & roll-ups

**Who:** E-commerce brand acquirers and beauty roll-ups.

**What they own:** Portfolios of acquired brands needing margin improvement and product
innovation.

**Why Cre8or:** Consolidates a fragmented supplier base across the portfolio, adds R&D
they don't have in-house, and improves working capital by removing inventory overhang.
**Cre8or already has a written position for this segment** ("Cre8or & Aggregators") —
reuse it rather than starting from zero.

## Segment E — Retailer private label & own-brand programmes

**Who:** Beauty and mass retailers running own-brand lines; off-price and value retail.

**What they own:** The shelf, and the sell-through data.

**Their problem:** Private-label development cycles are long, and testing a new own-brand
concept requires a committed volume buy.

**Why Cre8or:** POS-connected production fits unusually well here, because the retailer
*has* the POS data. Test a concept in a handful of doors at tiny volume; scale only what
sells. Arguably the purest expression of the Cre8orOS thesis.

`[ASSUMPTION]` Weakest on winnability — retailer vendor onboarding is slow and heavily
gated. High value, long cycle. Rank accordingly.

## Segment F — Distributors, travel retail & promotional products

**Who:** Regional beauty distributors, travel-retail operators, corporate gifting and
promotional-products firms (the ASI/PPAI channel).

**Why Cre8or:** Custom, low-volume, fast-turn branded product *is* the promotional
products business. Beauty is underserved in it, and the volume profile matches Cre8or's
model almost exactly.

`[ASSUMPTION]` Lowest deal value per partner, but likely the fastest to close — and the
best proving ground for the operating model before a marquee Segment A deal is at stake.

## How to rank these

Score each segment in `02-strategy/partner-tiers.md` on:

- **Volume potential** — lines per year × units per line
- **Winnability** — length and gatedness of the sales cycle
- **Strategic value** — does a logo here open the next three doors?
- **Operational fit** — can Cre8or service their SKU mix, geography and speed?
- **Brand risk** — what happens to Cre8or if their line fails publicly?

Do not rank before the research lands. The instinct is that Segment A is the prize and
B or F is the fastest first win — but that is a hypothesis, not a finding.
